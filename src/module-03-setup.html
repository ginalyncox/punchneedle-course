Module 03 · Setup & Design Transfer

Setup is unglamorous. It's also where most doomed projects are decided before the first loop. This module covers cutting and framing monks cloth, mirroring your design (a non-obvious requirement), and four transfer methods ranked by clarity and cost.

Lessons3
Reading time~15 min
MaterialsMonks cloth, frame, marker, printout

Lesson 3.1 — Cutting and preparing monks cloth

Cut your monks cloth generously. The rule: your design size plus a minimum 4" margin on every side, ideally 5–6". You need that margin to mount the fabric onto a frame with proper tension and to hem the finished piece.

  1. Measure and mark to the grid Monks cloth has visible warp and weft threads. Cut along the grain — pull a single thread and cut along the resulting line. This gives a straight, clean edge that won't bias-stretch when you frame it.
  2. Seal the raw edges Monks cloth ravels aggressively. Options: run a wide zigzag around the perimeter on a sewing machine, apply a strip of masking or duct tape around all four edges, or fold and iron a narrow hem. The tape method is fastest for course work — masking tape is fine, it comes off cleanly at the end.
  3. Iron before you mark Wrinkles distort your design. A quick press with a warm iron (no steam if you're worried about shrinkage) makes both transfer and framing significantly easier.

Lesson 3.2 — Mounting and tension

Everything from Module 01 comes back here: drum-tight or nothing. The mounting technique depends on your frame.

Embroidery hoop

  1. Lay the fabric over the inner ringDesign side up if you're using a see-through transfer method, or design side down if you've already drawn the mirrored version and will punch on the reverse.
  2. Press the outer ring on topScrew slightly, then work around the fabric edge pulling in every direction until the surface is drum-tight. Tighten the screw. Test with a flick — it should sound like a low drum.
  3. Re-tighten every 10–15 minutesFabric relaxes as you work. Punching itself pulls the weave slightly. Get in the habit of checking tension.

Gripper frame

  1. Center the fabric over the frameDesign side up (or mirrored side up if you're punching from the reverse).
  2. Press onto the gripper teethStart at the middle of one side. Press the fabric onto the metal teeth firmly with your palm. Move to the opposite side and pull the fabric taut before pressing down.
  3. Work around all four sides, corners lastThen go around a second time, adjusting anywhere that looks slack. Corners take a bit of coaxing — pull diagonally.
  4. Test the tensionFlick. Drum note. Good.

Stretcher bars with staples/tacks

Assemble the bars into a rectangle. Center the fabric, staple the middle of each side first, then work outward toward the corners, pulling taut as you go. Corner folds get stapled last. Once mounted, this can't be easily re-tensioned — get it right the first time.

Lesson 3.3 — Transferring your design

Before we discuss methods: punch needle is worked on the reverse of the finished piece. Whatever you punch, the loops appear on the other side. That means:

Mirror your design

Whatever asymmetry your design has — text, a leftward-tilting shape, a signature — must be reversed on the monks cloth. In print software, use a "flip horizontal" option before printing. If you skip this step, your finished rug will read as a mirror image of your source.

Four transfer methods, ranked from fastest to most permanent:

Method A — Light box or bright window

The simplest and most accurate for cream monks cloth.

  1. Print your mirrored design at final size on plain paper. Go over the lines with a thick black Sharpie so they show through the fabric.
  2. Tape the printout to a window (daytime) or a light box.
  3. Tape the monks cloth on top of the printout, aligned to your design position.
  4. Trace the visible lines onto the monks cloth with a fine permanent marker or water-soluble fabric marker.

Best for: flat pieces, small-to-medium designs, when you want precision.

Method B — Iron-on transfer pencil

A Sulky (or similar) iron-on transfer pencil lets you trace your design onto paper, then iron the paper onto the fabric.

  1. Print your design at final size (do not mirror — the iron-on step mirrors it for you). Trace over the lines with the iron-on transfer pencil.
  2. Place the paper pencil-side-down on your monks cloth.
  3. Press with a dry iron on medium heat for 5–10 seconds per section, following the pencil's instructions.
  4. Lift a corner to check the transfer, then peel the paper away.

Best for: intricate line work, pieces you want to reuse the pattern from.

Method C — Red dot tracing paper

A gridded transparent fabric used mainly by quilters. Trace your design onto the red dot paper with a fine marker, then layer it on top of your monks cloth and press hard with a permanent marker to bleed the ink through.

Best for: when a light box isn't available and iron-on isn't precise enough.

Method D — Freehand with the grid

Monks cloth's white grid stripes appear every 2 inches. For geometric or simple abstract designs, you can sketch directly onto the fabric using the grid as a coordinate system.

Best for: geometric compositions (stripes, checks, simple color blocks), sketching-oriented makers who want the imperfection.

Marker choice

Water-soluble fabric markers wash out if you finish-wash the piece — good if you want no ghost lines. Permanent fine-tip Sharpies are more reliable to see but will be covered by loops and are essentially invisible when finished. Do not use a marker that bleeds through the loops to the other side — test on scrap.
